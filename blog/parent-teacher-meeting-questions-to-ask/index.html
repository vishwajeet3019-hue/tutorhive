Parent-teacher meeting

Parent-Teacher Meeting: 15 Questions to Ask (and How to Use the Answers)

Most parent-teacher meetings last about five minutes, with a queue of other parents waiting behind you. The teacher says "he is a good boy, needs to concentrate more", you nod, and everyone goes home having learned very little. The meeting is not the problem. Walking in without questions is. A prepared parent can get genuinely useful information in those five minutes: where the marks are really going, how your child behaves in class, and the one thing worth changing first.

The short answer

Prepare three things, ask for examples instead of opinions, and leave with one action. Before you go, look at two or three corrected papers, write down your top three questions and ask your child what you should raise. In the room, ask "where exactly are the marks being lost?" rather than "how is he doing?". Before you leave, agree one thing to do at home, one thing the teacher will watch, and a date to check back.

Before the meeting: twenty minutes that change everything

  1. Read the report card as a pattern, not a total. Which subject has fallen, since when, and is it the same one as last term? Our guide to reading a half-yearly report card shows how.
  2. Look at two or three corrected papers or notebooks. Mark where the marks were lost and bring them. One real example is worth ten general impressions.
  3. Ask your child two questions. "What should I ask your teacher?" and "Is there anything you would like me to mention, or not mention?" Children often name the real issue straight away: a seat next to a distracting friend, one chapter they never understood, fear of asking in front of the class.
  4. Choose your top three questions. Five minutes is enough for three, not twelve. Pick them from the list below.
  5. Check the logistics. Will only the class teacher be there, or the subject teachers too? If the teacher you most need to see is not available, ask how to reach them, through the school diary, email or the school's official channel.

Go in aiming for a clearer picture, not a verdict. A parent who arrives curious gets far more honest information than one who arrives defensive.

Fifteen questions worth asking

You will not ask all of them. Pick three or four that fit your child, and keep the rest for the next meeting. Each comes with the reason it works.

Where my child stands

  1. "Which topics or skills is my child strongest and weakest in right now?" It gets you specifics instead of "average". A named weak topic can be fixed; "needs to work harder" cannot.
  2. "Are marks being lost because of understanding, carelessness or running out of time?" These three need completely different fixes, and teachers can usually tell from the papers.
  3. "How does this compare with what you expected from my child at this point in the year?" It judges progress against your child's own potential, not against a class rank.

In the classroom

  1. "Does my child ask questions or answer in class?" A silent child often has hidden gaps. Knowing whether they speak up tells you whether doubts are going unasked.
  2. "How do they get on with classmates, and who do they sit with?" Social problems and seating often show up first as falling marks.
  3. "Is their attention steady, or does it change by subject or time of day?" A pattern, such as drifting after lunch or in one subject only, points to a cause.

Work habits

  1. "Is homework complete, on time and clearly their own work?" It tells you whether what you see at home matches what the school sees.
  2. "How do they handle tests? Do they finish, and do they check their answers?" Unfinished papers and no checking are habits you can train at home.
  3. "Are their notebooks up to date?" Missing notes after an absence quietly become missing understanding.

What to do next

  1. "What is the one change that would help most over the next six weeks?" It forces a priority. One change that happens beats a list of ten that do not.
  2. "What should I do at home, specifically, and is there anything I should stop doing?" It shows you are a partner, and teachers will often gently mention over-helping or too much pressure.
  3. "Does the school run doubt-clearing, remedial or extra practice sessions?" Free help the school already offers is the first thing to use.

Looking ahead

  1. "What will the next assessment cover, and how are projects and internal marks weighted?" In CBSE Classes 9 and 10, 20 marks in each major subject come from internal assessment, so knowing how they are earned matters.
  2. "Is there anything about attention, eyesight, reading, speech or mood that you would like me to look into?" It invites the teacher to share a sensitive observation they might not otherwise raise.
  3. "Can we check in again in four to six weeks, and how is best to reach you?" It turns a one-off meeting into a loop, and gives you a way to ask the follow-up.

Decoding common teacher remarks

Teachers are juggling many students and often use short, polite phrases. Each one has a more useful question hiding behind it.

What you hearWhat it often meansWhat to ask next
"Needs to concentrate more"The teacher has noticed drifting but may not know why"In which lessons? Can you give me an example from this week?"
"Careless mistakes"Errors in copying, calculating or reading, which are different problems"Which kind is it? Could you show me one?"
"Can do better"A gap between ability and output, not yet defined"What would better look like: marks, finishing papers, or taking part?"
"Weak in basics"A gap from an earlier class"Which basics exactly, and from which class?"
"Talks a lot / gets distracted"Boredom, social issues or seating"When does it happen? Would a different seat help?"
"Shy, does not participate"Probably hiding doubts"What helps quieter children in your class speak up?"
"No problems at all" (but marks are falling)The issue may not be visible in class, or the teacher is being polite"Can I see where marks were lost in the last test? What do you notice in written work?"

How to conduct yourself in the room

  • Start with something you appreciate. One sincere sentence, such as "she says you explain things clearly", sets a cooperative tone for the next four minutes.
  • Ask for examples, not labels. "Can you show me" gets more than "is he lazy?".
  • Do not argue about individual marks. A queue is waiting. If you disagree with how a paper was marked, ask for a separate time.
  • Do not compare or ask for ranks. Ask about progress instead: is it improving, and where?
  • Take notes and repeat the action item back. "So I will practise timed sections and you will check how many questions she finishes?" avoids confusion later.

Should your child come along?

It depends on the school and the age. Where it is allowed, a short joint conversation can work well for older children, especially from Class 8 onwards, because they take ownership of the plan. For younger children, or when you need to discuss something sensitive, it is often better to meet the teacher alone and then share the useful parts afterwards. Decide beforehand and tell your child what to expect, so the meeting never feels like a trial.

Right after: the ten-minute debrief

The meeting only helps if something changes afterwards, and the most important conversation is the one with your child, ideally the same day. Start with a real positive the teacher mentioned, then one focus area, then ask for their view. For example: "Your teacher said you explain things really well in science. She thinks maths homework sometimes feels rushed. What do you think is going on?" Then listen.

Write down three lines while it is fresh, and share them with the other parent or whoever helps with studies:

What we learned: ...

One thing we will do at home, and for how long: ...

One thing the teacher will watch: ...

We check back on: (date)

Put the check-back date in your calendar. This single habit is what separates a meeting that changes things from one that only produces good intentions.

When the meeting is five minutes, or online

Many meetings are short, and some schools now hold them online. In both cases, send your top three questions in advance through the school's official channel, so the teacher can think about them. Ask whether a longer slot or a short call is possible if you have a bigger concern. And ask at the end whether it would be better to continue by email or message, so the conversation does not end at the door.

Signs that need more than a PTM

Some things should not wait for the next scheduled meeting. If you notice a sudden change in behaviour, your child avoiding school, comments about being teased or left out, or a big unexplained drop in marks, ask the class teacher in writing for a meeting, and request that the school counsellor or coordinator be included. Keep a short record of dates and what was said.

If your child seems persistently low, anxious or withdrawn, speak to the school counsellor or a doctor. In India, Tele-MANAS, the national mental health helpline, can be reached on 14416. Getting help early is routine and sensible, not an overreaction. For everyday exam pressure, our guide on Class 10 board exam stress has practical steps.

Using the timing well

Meetings held after half-yearly or term results are the most valuable, because there is fresh evidence on the table and time to act before the next exam. If your child is in Class 9 or 10, the same logic applies to the pre-board exams. If marks have already begun to fall, read why marks drop in Class 6, 7 and 8 before the meeting, and if motivation is the worry, what to do when a child is not interested in studying.

If the teacher points to gaps and you consider extra help

A good meeting gives you something tuition decisions usually lack: the teacher's own examples of where marks are being lost. Pass these on to any tutor, so their first sessions target the real weak spots and not a generic revision. Ask them what they expect to change in six to eight weeks and how they will tell you. Frequently asked questions

How long does a parent-teacher meeting usually last?

In many schools it is only five to ten minutes per family, which is why preparing three focused questions matters more than trying to cover everything. If you have a bigger concern, ask for a separate appointment rather than using up the queue's time.

How do I ask about low marks without sounding critical of the teacher?

Phrase it as curiosity about the child, not about the teaching. "Where do you see the marks being lost?" or "What do you notice in his written work?" invites information, while "why is she scoring so low?" invites defence. Bringing a corrected paper also keeps the conversation on specifics.

Should my child attend the parent-teacher meeting?

Where the school allows it, older children, especially from Class 8 onwards, often benefit from joining for part of the meeting because they own the plan afterwards. For younger children or sensitive topics, meeting alone first and sharing the useful parts afterwards usually works better. Tell your child beforehand what to expect.

What if the teacher seems dismissive or has little to say?

Ask for something concrete, such as the last test paper or two examples of where marks were lost. If you still do not get useful answers, request a separate short appointment, send written questions through the school's official channel, or speak to the coordinator. Many teachers have a full queue and will give more time when asked in advance.

What if I disagree with the teacher's view of my child?

Do not argue it out in the meeting. Ask for examples, note them, and compare with what you see at home and in your child's papers. If you still disagree, request a follow-up. Often both views are partly right, because children behave differently in class and at home.

How often should I speak to my child's teachers apart from scheduled meetings?

A short check-in once a term is a reasonable rhythm, plus any time something changes, such as a sudden drop in marks, a change in behaviour or a long absence. Short written messages through the school's official channel are often more effective than waiting for the next meeting.
