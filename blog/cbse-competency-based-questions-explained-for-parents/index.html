CBSE Competency-Based Questions: What They Are and How to Help Your Child Prepare

Your child knows the chapter. They can recite the definition, repeat the steps and finish the textbook exercise. Then the test arrives with a question that looks nothing like the textbook, and the marks quietly disappear. That is almost always a competency-based question, and it is now a large part of how CBSE tests students. Here is what these questions are, what CBSE's own 2026-27 documents say about the 2027 Class 10 boards, and how to prepare at home without adding hours.

The short answer

A competency-based question tests whether your child can use what they learned in a situation they have not seen before, instead of repeating it. CBSE's 2026-27 curriculum says that approximately 50% of the questions in Board Examination papers are competency-focused: case-based, source-based, integrated, data interpretation, situational and application-oriented. The chapters being tested are the ones in the syllabus. What changes is how they are asked, so the fix is practising unfamiliar questions and explaining reasoning, not studying more hours.

What a competency-based question actually is

A traditional question asks your child to recall: state a law, define a term, write a formula, reproduce a method. A competency-based question asks them to use the same idea: apply it to a real situation, pull information out of a passage or table, choose between options and justify the choice, or connect two chapters. The topic is identical. The thinking required is not.

CBSE describes the aim itself as a "deliberate shift from rote learning to competency-based education" in its Class 10 Maths curriculum for 2026-27. In practice, that shift is what your child feels as "the paper had questions we never practised". Here is the same idea asked both ways in each of the four main subjects.

Maths Class 10, Arithmetic Progressions

Recall Find the 20th term of the AP 50, 65, 80, ...

Competency A taxi charges ₹50 for the first kilometre and ₹15 for every kilometre after that. Anaya's fare was ₹320. How many kilometres did she travel? Could a fare of ₹400 ever appear on the meter? Explain.

The second version hides an AP inside a story. Your child has to spot it, solve it (19 km), and then reason that (400 − 50) ÷ 15 is not a whole number, so ₹400 cannot appear. The method is the same; the recognising and the reasoning are the new parts.

Science Class 10, Electricity

Recall State Ohm's law.

Competency Wires P and Q are connected, one at a time, to the same 4 V battery. P carries 1.0 A and Q carries 0.5 A. Which wire has the greater resistance, and by what factor? If the two wires have the same length and are made of the same material, which one is thicker, and why?

Here the law is used on data (P is 4 Ω, Q is 8 Ω, so Q has twice the resistance), and then linked to a second idea: for the same length and material, the thicker wire has the lower resistance, so P is thicker.

Social Science Class 10, Money and Credit

Recall Name two sources of formal credit.

Competency A small farmer needs ₹30,000 before the sowing season. A local moneylender offers it immediately at a high rate of interest. The bank offers a lower rate, but needs collateral and paperwork that will take weeks. Which would you advise, and why? What does this show about who can actually use formal credit?

There is no single memorised answer. Marks go to weighing the trade-offs (speed, interest, collateral, documents) using what the chapter teaches about formal and informal credit.

English Reading comprehension with a chart (figures invented for illustration)

A passage on student sleep comes with a chart showing the share of students who sleep 8 hours or more: Class 6, 78%; Class 7, 64%; Class 8, 49%. Which statement does the data support?

(a) Older students care less about sleep. (b) The share of students getting enough sleep falls as the class rises. (c) Class 8 students study more than Class 6 students. (d) Poor sleep lowers marks.

The answer is (b). The other three may sound sensible, but the chart does not show them. The skill being tested is reading carefully and not claiming more than the evidence says. CBSE's Class 10 English curriculum includes a case-based factual passage with a visual input such as a chart or statistical data in the reading section.

The same shift reaches the middle school too. Recall: divide 3/4 by 12. Competency: a recipe uses 3/4 cup of sugar for 12 laddoos; how much sugar is needed for 20 laddoos, and how did you decide? (It is 1¼ cups.) A child who has only practised the bare sum often stalls on the laddoos.

What the official numbers say for the 2027 boards

There are two separate facts in CBSE's 2026-27 curriculum, and they are easy to mix up.

First, the format. Section 3.1 of the curriculum says Board Examination papers are designed to assess application, analysis and evaluation, and that "approximately 50% of the questions are competency-focused", such as case-based, source-based, integrated, data interpretation, situational and application-oriented questions. The remaining items are select-response (MCQs) and constructed-response (short and long answer) questions. CBSE adds that this structure may be reviewed periodically.

Second, the thinking level. Each subject's question paper design splits its 80 theory marks by how demanding the thinking is. Here is what the Class 10 designs for 2026-27 show:

Maths (Standard)43 marks19 marks18 marks-

Source: the question paper designs in CBSE's Class 10 curriculum for 2026-27. CBSE gives Science as percentages; the marks shown are those percentages applied to 80. Each subject also carries 20 marks of internal assessment awarded by the school.

Three things stand out when you read the table as a parent.

  • Social Science carries the most higher-order marks. Half the paper (40 of 80) sits at analysing, evaluating and creating, and the design also lists three case-study questions of 4 marks each. A child who has memorised the NCERT text but never practised arguing from a source can lose a surprising amount here.
  • Science puts half its marks above recall. Application and higher-order questions together make 40 of 80 marks.
  • Maths is still mostly recall and understanding, but not entirely. In the Standard paper, 18 marks need analysis and judgement. Those are the marks most likely to separate a 90 from an 80, so a student aiming high cannot afford to skip them.

One caution: the 50% in the curriculum counts question formats, while the table counts marks by thinking level. They measure different things, so do not try to add them up. Check the latest sample paper and marking scheme on the CBSE academic site before the exam, because CBSE says the structure can be reviewed.

What about Class 9 and Classes 6 to 8?

For Class 9, CBSE's curriculum says the assessment scheme will be similar to the Class 10 board examination, and the Class 9 Maths curriculum lists the same paper design as Class 10. But Class 9 papers are school-based, so what your child actually sees depends on the school. For Classes 6 to 8 there is no board paper at all, yet many schools set tests in this style because that is where students are heading. The most reliable evidence is not a national figure but your child's last two corrected tests: look at which questions lost marks.

The question types your child will meet

It helps to give your child the vocabulary, because an unfamiliar format on its own is a common reason for freezing.

  • Case-based. A short scenario, with data, followed by several linked questions. Social Science has three of these worth 4 marks each in the 2026-27 design.
  • Source-based. An extract from a document, speech, picture or map, with questions that must be answered using it.
  • Data interpretation. Tables, graphs, charts and maps. What changed, by how much, and what might explain it?
  • Assertion and reason. Two statements; the student decides whether each is true and whether the reason actually explains the assertion. CBSE's Science paper design lists this type explicitly.
  • Situational and application. A real-life scenario, such as the taxi fare above, where the student must decide which idea applies.
  • Integrated. Questions that need ideas from more than one chapter or subject.

Assertion and reason Science, Class 10

Assertion (A): An iron nail dipped in copper sulphate solution gets a brownish coating.

Reason (R): Iron is more reactive than copper and displaces it from its solution.

(a) Both A and R are true, and R is the correct explanation of A. (b) Both A and R are true, but R is not the correct explanation of A. (c) A is true, but R is false. (d) A is false, but R is true.

The answer is (a). A child who only memorised "iron displaces copper" may still hesitate between (a) and (b), because the question is really asking whether the second statement explains the first.

Why capable children still lose marks on these

  1. They studied to recognise, not to reproduce. Re-reading and highlighting make a chapter feel familiar, but familiar is not the same as usable.
  2. Long questions overwhelm them. With a paragraph and a table in front of them, they cannot tell which details matter. This is a reading-stamina problem as much as a subject problem.
  3. An unfamiliar context freezes them. "We never did this in class" usually means the underlying idea is the same and the story is different. They have not learned to look through the story.
  4. They stop at the answer. Questions that say "explain", "justify" or "give a reason" award marks for the reasoning. A correct answer with no because loses them.
  5. Time runs out. Case-based sets take longer to read, so the last section is rushed or left incomplete.

A ten-minute check you can do tonight

You do not need to know the subject. You only need a textbook the child has already studied.

  1. Pick a solved example from a chapter your child says they know well.
  2. Keep the idea but change the numbers or the story. Ask them to solve the new version out loud.
  3. Watch where they stall. If they cannot start, they do not yet know which idea applies. If they start and go wrong, they memorised the steps but not the reason. If they get it right but cannot say why, ask "how do you know?" and listen.

A child who can do the textbook version but not the changed one is a recall learner. That is extremely common and very fixable, and it is far better to find out in October than on the day of the pre-board.

How to prepare at home without adding hours

  1. Replace some re-reading with twist questions. After each chapter, ask your child to write three questions about it in a new setting, then swap and answer each other's. Writing a good question needs more understanding than answering one.
  2. Use the right practice material. CBSE lists the NCERT exemplar problems as prescribed practice for Class 10 Maths and Science. Add CBSE sample papers with marking schemes and last year's papers. Be careful with old guide books and "important question" lists: many were built around recall.
  3. Teach a given-find-link routine. For any long question, underline what is given, circle what is asked, and name the idea that connects them before writing a single line.
  4. Read data for five minutes a day. A chart from the newspaper, a table in the textbook, a map. Ask: what changed, by how much, and what might explain it?
  5. Always write the because. One sentence of reasoning, with the unit or a closing line, on every answer that says explain, justify or give a reason.
  6. Time the case-based sets. Teach your child to read the questions first and the passage second, so they read with a purpose. Practise with a timer.
  7. Keep an error log by type. Mark each lost mark as did not know, misread, picked the wrong idea or careless. Fix the type, not just the question. Our guides on study tips toppers use and board exam answer writing show how.

Four myths worth dropping

  • "Competency-based means a tougher syllabus." No. The chapters are the same ones. The difference is in how the questions are asked.
  • "A list of important questions will cover it." Lists built on recall miss the changed contexts, which is the whole point of these questions.
  • "Only Maths and Science students need to worry." In CBSE's own design, Social Science has the largest share of higher-order marks.
  • "It is only for toppers." The top-level marks matter most to students aiming for 90 and above, but application marks, which run from roughly a quarter to nearly a third of the paper in Maths and Science, matter to everyone.

If your child is in Classes 6 to 9

Start light and start now. One twist question a week per subject, praise for explaining a method rather than finishing fast, and a habit of asking "why does that work?" at the dinner table will do more than any amount of last-minute drilling in Class 10. Where tuition fits

Tuition helps with competency-based questions only if it teaches the idea behind the method and then makes your child apply it in unfamiliar settings. Re-teaching the chapter in the same way the school did, or handing over more recall worksheets, will not move these marks. A simple test during any demo class is to ask the tutor to take one chapter and show how they would teach the twisted version.

Frequently asked questions

What does competency-based mean in CBSE?

It means the question tests whether a student can apply what they have learned, not just repeat it. Instead of asking for a definition or a formula, the question places the idea in a real situation, a passage, a data table or a map, and asks the student to reason with it. CBSE's curriculum describes the broader aim as a shift from rote learning to competency-based education.

What percentage of the CBSE Class 10 paper is competency-based in 2027?

CBSE's 2026-27 curriculum says that approximately 50% of the questions in Board Examination papers are competency-focused, such as case-based, source-based, integrated, data interpretation, situational and application-oriented questions. The rest are MCQs and short- and long-answer questions. CBSE also notes that this structure may be reviewed periodically, so check the latest sample paper.

Are competency-based questions harder than normal questions?

They are less predictable rather than strictly harder. A student who understands the idea usually finds them manageable, because the context is new but the concept is familiar. A student who has memorised answers finds them hard because there is no memorised answer to retrieve. That is why the best preparation is understanding and varied practice, not extra hours.

Do competency-based questions apply to Classes 6 to 9?

There is no board exam before Class 10, so papers are set by schools. CBSE's curriculum says the Class 9 assessment scheme will be similar to Class 10, and many schools set tests in this style for Classes 6 to 9 as preparation. Your child's own corrected tests are the best guide to what their school actually asks.

How many marks in Class 10 Maths need higher-order thinking?

In CBSE's 2026-27 design for Maths (Standard), 18 of the 80 theory marks are for analysing, evaluating and creating, and another 19 are for applying what has been learned. Together that is 37 marks above plain recall and understanding. In Maths (Basic) the figures are 8 and 12.

Where can my child practise competency-based questions?

Start with the NCERT exemplar problems, which CBSE lists as prescribed practice for Class 10 Maths and Science, then solve the CBSE sample papers and marking schemes that the board releases on cbseacademic.nic.in, followed by previous years' papers under a timer. Add everyday data reading, such as a newspaper chart, and ask your child to explain their answers out loud.
