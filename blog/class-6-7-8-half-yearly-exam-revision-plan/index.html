Half-yearly exams, Classes 6-8

Class 6, 7 and 8 Half-Yearly Exams: A Four-Week, Chapter-Wise Revision Plan for Parents

Half-yearly exams are where many Class 6, 7 and 8 parents first feel the panic: the portion looks huge, the child says "I know everything" and then forgets half of it, and every subject seems to need attention at once. The good news is that the middle school books are built around a limited number of chapters per subject, so a calm, chapter-by-chapter plan works far better than a last-week scramble. This guide gives you a four-week plan, a simple way to decide which chapters need the most time, and subject-wise methods that fit NCERT's 2026-27 books.

The short answer

Start four weeks before the exam. First get the exact portion from the school, list its chapters, and rate each one green, amber or red. Revise the red chapters first using recall (closing the book and explaining), practise amber ones with timed questions, and keep the last two days light. Do not try to relearn everything. The aim is to find and fix the weakest chapters while there is still time. Ask the school for the portion, because each school decides which chapters its half-yearly covers.

Step 1: get the portion in writing

Each school decides which chapters its half-yearly exam covers, and the dates. Before you plan anything, ask your child's class teacher or check the school diary or app for the portion, the exam timetable and the paper pattern. Then write the chapters in a simple table, one per subject, like this.

Step 2: rate every chapter green, amber or red

Do this honestly and quickly, in about an hour with your child. For each chapter in the portion, ask them to explain the main idea in two or three sentences without opening the book, or solve two questions from the end of the chapter.

  • Green: they explain it or solve it easily. A quick recall later is enough.
  • Amber: they get most of it but hesitate or make mistakes. Needs timed practice.
  • Red: they cannot explain it or get stuck. Needs the book re-read, an example worked together and then practice.

This one hour tells you where the next four weeks should go. Most children turn out to have a handful of red chapters, not an entire subject. If red chapters keep appearing in the same subject, it may point to a gap from an earlier class; our guide on why marks drop in Classes 6 to 8 explains how to find it.

Step 3: the four-week plan

WeekFocusWhat to do
Week 1Plan and fix the redsGet the portion, rate chapters, collect notes and corrected tests. Start on the red chapters, one or two a day, in the subject that worries you most.
Week 2Finish the reds, start the ambersRe-read, work examples, then close the book and explain. Start timed practice on amber chapters.
Week 3Practise like the examDo timed sections from each subject. Mark them with your child and write down every lost mark in an error log (did not know, misread, careless, ran out of time).
Week 4Mock paper and light revisionOne full mock paper in each subject under exam timing. Fix the errors it shows. In the last two days revise only the error log and key points, and sleep well.

For a typical Class 6 to 8 child, an hour to an hour and a half a day of focused revision is a sensible rough guide, on top of normal homework. Sleep matters more than an extra hour: tired children recall less. Our wider guide to half-yearly exam preparation for Classes 6 to 10 has more on routine and parent support.

Maths: practise, do not just read

Maths is not learned by reading. The best revision is to redo questions from the end of each chapter without looking at the solution, then check. A few chapter-aware ideas follow.

  • Class 6: Fractions and The Other Side of Zero (negative numbers) are new ideas, and Prime Time needs fast multiplication and division facts. Spend extra time on these three.
  • Class 7: Algebra begins here (Expressions using Letter-Numbers in Part I and Finding the Unknown in Part II), and NCERT notes that congruence and central tendency are new to students. Revise these with worked examples and then fresh questions.
  • Class 8: Algebra appears in two places (the distributive law in Part I and Algebra Play in Part II), and proportional reasoning runs across both parts. These are good chapters to practise with mixed questions.
  • For every class: keep a one-page formula sheet, and an error log of every mistake with one line on why it happened.

Science: explain the activity

NCERT's Science books are built around activities and questions, so questions often ask why something happens, not just what it is called. For each red or amber chapter, ask your child to explain one activity from it in their own words, draw the key diagram from memory and write the important terms with one-line meanings. Class 6's Curiosity has 12 chapters, Class 7's has 12 and Class 8's has 13, so a chapter a day for the red ones is realistic. See our science revision tips.

Social Science: maps, timelines and short answers

Social Science has the longest portion, so it is where a weekly habit pays off. For each chapter, make a one-page summary with three to five key points, a small timeline for history chapters, and practise locating places on a map for geography chapters. Practise writing answers as three to five clear points rather than long paragraphs. Our social studies study tips show how.

English: read, retell and write

For the lessons in Poorvi, ask your child to retell each one in four or five sentences, say what the main message was and explain two difficult words. For writing, practise the formats the school uses, such as a letter or a short paragraph, in a set time. For grammar, practise from the school's own worksheets. Our guides on reading comprehension and English writing give routines.

Expect some application-style questions

Many schools now include questions that ask children to apply an idea in a new situation, in line with CBSE's competency-focused approach. To practise, change the numbers or the story in a textbook question and see whether your child can still solve it. Our guide to competency-based questions explains the idea.

How parents can help without adding pressure

  • Protect sleep and meals. Revision after a bad night is mostly wasted.
  • Keep a visible plan. A one-page timetable on the wall is better than reminders.
  • Ask, do not quiz. "Explain this chapter to me" works better than "Why have you not finished?".
  • Do not compare. Another child's schedule tells you nothing about yours.
  • Plan breaks. A short walk or play after each study block keeps energy up. See our Diwali and winter break study plan for how to protect the holidays.

Frequently asked questions

How early should I start preparing for half-yearly exams?

About four weeks before the exam is a good target. That is enough time to rate the chapters, fix the weak ones with recall and practice, and do a mock paper, without turning the weeks before the exam into a scramble.

How many hours a day should a Class 6 to 8 child revise?

As a rough guide, an hour to an hour and a half of focused revision a day, on top of normal homework, is enough for most children. The quality of that time matters more than the number of hours, and sleep matters more than an extra hour of study.

What if the portion is very large?

Rate the chapters green, amber and red and spend most of your time on the red ones. Green chapters need only a quick recall. A large portion feels smaller once you can see where the real gaps are.

Should we hire a tutor just before the exam?

A tutor can help if they focus on the specific chapters that are red and use the time for explanation and practice. A general re-teach just before an exam is rarely worth it. A short trial class can show whether the tutor can find the gap quickly.

My child says they know everything but forgets in the test. What should I do?

This usually means they recognise the chapter but cannot recall it. Replace re-reading with recall: close the book, explain the chapter aloud or write it out, then check. Timed practice questions also expose gaps that re-reading hides.

What if my child panics before exams?

Keep routines steady, protect sleep and break the work into small pieces. Talk about the plan rather than the result. If anxiety is strong or lasts more than a couple of weeks, speak to the school counsellor. In India, Tele-MANAS, the national mental health helpline, can be reached on 14416.
