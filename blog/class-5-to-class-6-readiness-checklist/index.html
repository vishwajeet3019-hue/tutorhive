Is Your Class 5 Child Ready for Class 6? A 12-Point Readiness Checklist for Parents

The move from Class 5 to Class 6 is where many children who were doing well start to wobble. Separate subject teachers, longer chapters, more writing and a new pace arrive together. The good news is that readiness has very little to do with how many marks your child scored in Class 5. It comes down to a handful of skills that Class 6 quietly assumes, and most of them can be checked at the kitchen table in ten minutes. This guide shows what Class 6 expects, using NCERT's own Class 6 books, and gives you a checklist with a simple way to test each skill and a gentle way to fix gaps.

The short answer

Class 6 assumes five kinds of readiness: quick multiplication and division facts, comfortable early fractions, reading a simple table or graph, reading a page and explaining it in your own words, and writing a short connected paragraph. Add basic independence: packing a bag, tracking homework and saying when something is not understood. Use the checklist below during Class 5. Fix gaps with five to fifteen minutes a day, not by rushing ahead into Class 6 chapters.

The 12-point readiness checklist

For each skill there is a quick check and a gentle fix. Do not test everything on one day. Pick two or three a weekend and keep it light, like a game.

Maths (five skills)

  1. Multiplication tables up to 10. Check: ask ten random facts such as 7 x 8 and 9 x 6 and see how many are right in a minute. Fix: five minutes a day on only the hardest tables, usually 6, 7, 8 and 9. Skip the ones they already know.
  2. Division as the reverse of multiplication. Check: "How many 6s are in 54?" and "What is 56 divided by 7?". Fix: practise fact families together: 7 x 8 = 56, 56 divided by 8 = 7.
  3. Large numbers and place value. Check: ask your child to read and write a number like 4,05,230 and say which digit is in which place. Fix: number cards or a place value chart on the fridge.
  4. Early fractions. Check: ask them to show 1/2, 1/4 and 3/4 on a drawing, and which is bigger, 1/2 or 1/3. Fix: fold paper, cut a roti or a chocolate bar into equal parts, and talk about what you see.
  5. Reading a table or simple graph. Check: show a bar graph from a newspaper and ask which bar is tallest and by how much. Fix: one chart a week at breakfast.

English (three skills)

  1. Reading a page aloud smoothly. Check: listen to a page of an ordinary story. Note the stumbles and skipped words. Fix: ten minutes of reading aloud together every day, taking turns.
  2. Retelling what was read. Check: after a short passage, ask for the main idea in three or four sentences: who, what, why. Fix: make this a habit after every story. See our guide to reading comprehension.
  3. Writing five or six connected sentences. Check: ask for a short paragraph on a simple topic in ten minutes. Look for capital letters, full stops and sentences that follow each other. Fix: three sentences a day about something that happened, then build up. Our guide on English writing skills has more ideas.

Curiosity and general awareness (two skills)

  1. Explaining an everyday "why". Check: ask why ice melts, or why the fan feels cool. Listen for their reasoning, not the textbook answer. Fix: one "why" a day at dinner, with no marks and no pressure.
  2. Finding a place on a map. Check: ask them to find your city, your state and one place they have visited on a map. Fix: keep a map on the wall and use it when planning trips or reading news.

Independence (two skills)

  1. Managing the school bag and homework. Check: for a week, step back and see how often they need reminding. Fix: a short checklist on the study table, ticked by them, not by you.
  2. Saying when they do not understand. Check: ask "what was the hardest part of today?" and see whether you get a real answer. Fix: praise the question, not just the right answer. A child who learns to hide confusion in Class 5 can carry that habit into Class 6.

How to use the checklist without turning it into pressure

  • Keep it playful. A weekend quiz with a small treat for effort works better than a test that feels like an exam.
  • Tick what is solid and ignore it. Many children will already have most of the twelve in place. Spend your time on the two or three that are not.
  • Fix one thing at a time. Five to fifteen minutes a day on a single skill beats an hour on three.
  • Do not teach ahead. Pre-teaching Class 6 chapters can confuse a child before the school introduces them in its own way. NCERT's Class 6 books are activity-based, so a child with solid basics will do better than one who has memorised early chapters.
  • Use the long break. The weeks before April are a good, calm time to work on one or two gaps.

What else changes in Class 6

  • Subject teachers. Instead of one class teacher, your child may have a different teacher for each subject, each seeing many more students. Our guide to why marks drop in Classes 6 to 8 explains the transition and how to spot trouble early.
  • A third language. CBSE's 2026-27 curriculum makes a third language (R3) compulsory from Class 6, with two or three periods a week focused on speaking and reading fluency. It is assessed by the school. Ask the Class 6 section which language it will use. Our three-language policy guide has the details.
  • More independence expected. Teachers check notebooks less closely, so habits you build now matter more.

Where tuition fits

Most children can get through this transition with steady home practice. If several checklist items are weak, or practice at home turns into arguments, a patient tutor for a short period can help, especially for tables, fractions and written English. Frequently asked questions

Is the jump from Class 5 to Class 6 really big?

For many children it is noticeable. Subject teachers, separate books, longer chapters and more writing arrive together, and a third language is added in CBSE schools. Children with solid basics and a habit of asking questions often adjust within a term, while those with small gaps can struggle at first.

What should my child know before starting Class 6?

The essentials are quick multiplication and division facts, early fractions, reading a simple table or graph, reading a page and retelling it, writing a short paragraph, and managing their own bag and homework. The checklist above breaks these into twelve skills with quick tests.

Should I start teaching Class 6 chapters early?

Usually not. NCERT's Class 6 books are activity-based, and early pre-teaching can confuse a child before the school introduces a topic. It is better to fix the basics, read widely and keep the habit of asking questions.

What if my child is weak in multiplication tables?

This is the most common gap and the easiest to fix. Practise only the hardest tables, 6, 7, 8 and 9, for five minutes a day, and link multiplication with division as fact families. Many children improve noticeably within a few weeks of short daily practice.

Does this checklist apply to ICSE and other boards?

Yes, the skills are board-neutral. The NCERT chapter names mentioned apply to CBSE schools, so for an ICSE or other board school, ask for the Class 6 book list and the school's syllabus.

When should I start preparing for Class 6?

Any time during Class 5 is fine, and a calm, light start now gives you time to fix gaps before the new session. The weeks before April are also a good window for one or two focused skills.
