CBSE Class 10 Study Timetable: A Realistic Daily Plan That Works

Most Class 10 timetables fail in the first week because they are built for a student who has 8 free hours a day and never gets tired. This is a plan built for a real school day: a fixed number of focused hours, five subjects rotated so nothing gets ignored, and a structure you can adjust as the board exams get closer.

First, be honest about how many hours you actually have

After school, coaching, travel, meals and some rest, most Class 10 students realistically have two to three hours of good focus left on a normal day. That is enough. A timetable that demands five or six hours every school night looks impressive on paper and gets abandoned by Friday. Plan for the hours you will genuinely sit down for, keep them consistent, and use holidays and Sundays for the longer four-to-six-hour stretches. Steady daily study across the whole week beats one heroic Sunday followed by five empty days.

The five-subject problem: rotate, don't cram

CBSE Class 10 has five main subjects — Maths, Science, Social Science, English and your second language (Hindi, Sanskrit or another). The mistake almost everyone makes is studying whichever subject has homework due tomorrow, which means the subject you are weakest at quietly gets ignored for weeks. Fix this by ranking your subjects from weakest to strongest based on your last two school tests. The weakest two or three get a slot almost every day. The stronger subjects rotate through the week — two or three sessions each is enough to keep them sharp.

A sample timetable for a school day

Adjust the times to your own schedule, but keep the shape: one subject per block, a short break between blocks, and the hardest subject when your mind is freshest.

4:00 – 4:20 pm — Snack and reset after school
4:20 – 5:10 pm — Weakest subject (e.g. Maths): new concept + 8–10 practice sums
5:10 – 5:25 pm — Break, move away from the desk
5:25 – 6:10 pm — Second weak subject (e.g. Science): one topic, then answer 5 textbook questions
6:10 – 7:30 pm — School homework, dinner, family time
8:00 – 8:40 pm — Rotating subject for the day (SST / English / second language): read + make short notes
8:40 – 9:00 pm — Same-day revision: quickly re-read what you studied today and close the book

That is roughly two and a half hours of real study, plus homework. The last 20-minute revision block is the most valuable part and the one students skip most often — reviewing material the same day is what moves it from "I understood it in class" to "I can reproduce it in an exam".

A sample timetable for a Sunday or holiday

8:30 – 10:00 am — Weakest subject: finish a full chapter or a full exercise set
10:00 – 10:30 am — Break
10:30 – 12:00 pm — Second subject: previous years' or sample-paper questions on chapters already done
12:00 – 3:00 pm — Lunch and a proper rest
3:00 – 4:15 pm — Third subject: revision of older chapters, not just the newest one
4:15 – 4:45 pm — Break
4:45 – 5:45 pm — Weekly review: update your chapter tracker, list every doubt from the week to ask in school or tuition

Keep one weekend afternoon or one full evening a week genuinely free. A timetable with no rest built in is a timetable you will start resenting.

How to build your own timetable in 20 minutes

One, write your five subjects and rank them weakest to strongest from your last two tests. Two, pick a fixed daily start time you can keep on most days — the habit matters more than the exact hour. Three, give your weakest two subjects a daily slot and put the other three on a rotation across the week. Four, make every block one subject only, ending with a few practice questions rather than more reading. Five, mark one lighter day. Six, write it on paper and stick it where you study. Seven, review and adjust it on the first of every month based on your latest marks.

Adjusting the plan as the boards get closer

A Class 10 timetable is not one fixed document for the whole year — it changes shape each term. Through the first term, the goal is simply keeping pace with school and closing gaps carried over from Class 9. From roughly November into December, shift to a first complete revision pass so every chapter has been properly seen at least twice before the pre-boards. January is for solving previous years' CBSE papers and sample papers under timed conditions, one subject at a time, to build speed and exam temperament — see the CBSE Class 10 sample papers practice plan for how to run each paper. In February and March, between papers, the plan should be short, calm revision of your formula and definition sheets and enough sleep before each exam — not new chapters. Making revision actually stick

Reading a chapter again is the weakest form of revision. Stronger methods take the same time and work far better: close the book and write down everything you remember about a topic, then check what you missed; solve questions without looking at worked examples first; explain a concept out loud as if teaching it; and keep one running sheet per subject of every formula, definition, date or diagram you tend to forget. Revisit that sheet weekly. By January it becomes your fastest revision tool.

If you've already fallen behind

Falling behind in one or two subjects is normal by mid-year and completely recoverable if you act now instead of waiting for the pre-board panic. For the first two weeks, give your daily slots entirely to clearing backlog in your weakest subject — earlier chapters first, since later chapters usually build on them. Once you are roughly caught up to where your class is, switch to the full rotation above. Do not try to fix three subjects at once; you will make no visible progress in any of them and give up. One subject, two weeks, then the next.

Frequently asked questions

How many hours a day should a CBSE Class 10 student study?

On a school day, about two to three focused hours at home is realistic and sustainable for most students, on top of school and coaching. On holidays and Sundays, four to six hours split into blocks with breaks works well. The number of hours matters far less than whether those hours are active practice rather than passive reading, and consistency across the week beats occasional long sessions.

How do I make a study timetable for Class 10 board exams?

List your five subjects, mark the two or three that are weakest, and give those the daily slots while the stronger subjects rotate through the week. Fix a same-time daily study block so it becomes a habit, keep one subject per block, end each block with a few practice questions, and leave one lighter day a week. Review the timetable every month and shift time toward whatever your latest tests show is weak.

Is it too late to start preparing for Class 10 boards mid-year?

No. If several months remain before the pre-boards, there is enough time to cover gaps if you start a steady daily routine now instead of waiting for the exam rush. Begin by clearing backlog in your weakest subject for two weeks, then move to a full rotation. Starting late with consistency beats starting early and stopping.

How should a Class 10 study timetable change before the board exams?

Through the first term it should focus on keeping pace with school and closing Class 9 gaps. From November to December it shifts to a first full revision pass across every chapter. January is for previous years' papers and sample papers under timed conditions. In February and March, between papers, the plan becomes short calm revision of formula and definition sheets plus enough sleep before each exam, not new topics.
