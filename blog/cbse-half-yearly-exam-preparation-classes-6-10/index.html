CBSE Half-Yearly Exam Preparation: A Complete Study Plan for Classes 6-10

The half-yearly exam is the first big test of the school year. Unlike a unit test, it covers everything taught in the last two terms - five to eight chapters per subject - and it carries real weight in the report card. Left until the last two weeks, that much syllabus feels impossible. Started six weeks out with a chapter-by-chapter plan, it is completely manageable. Here is how to prepare for it without the last-minute panic.

Why the half-yearly exam deserves its own plan

Most schools that begin the academic year in April hold the half-yearly exam in November or December, after two terms of teaching. By then, a student has covered enough ground that "revise everything the night before" simply stops working. This is different from a unit test, which usually covers one or two chapters and can be managed with a few focused evenings.

Three things make the half-yearly worth preparing for deliberately rather than treating like a bigger unit test. First, the syllabus is large enough that early chapters have often gone cold by the time revision starts - the student "did" them in June but has not touched them since. Second, most schools weight the half-yearly more heavily than a single periodic test when calculating the term or annual report. Third, for Class 9 and 10 students, the half-yearly is often the first paper set in a board-style pattern - case-based questions, longer answers, a proper reading-time structure - making it useful practice for the boards ahead, not just a school formality.

Start by mapping the syllabus, not the textbook

Before opening a single chapter, write down - on paper or in a note - every chapter included in the half-yearly, subject by subject, exactly as the school's syllabus notice lists them. Most students skip this step and instead flip through the textbook from the start, which wastes time on chapters that already feel solid and leaves too little time for the ones that don't.

Once the list exists, sort each chapter into three buckets: clear (can explain it without notes), shaky (recognises it but makes mistakes), and weak (needs to be relearned close to from scratch). This ten-minute exercise does more for a revision plan than any generic timetable, because it tells you exactly where the six weeks should go - a light touch on the clear chapters, focused practice on the shaky ones, and real teaching time on the weak ones.

A realistic six-week plan

This structure works whether the exam is six weeks away or slightly less - compress the weeks rather than skipping steps if time is shorter.

Weeks 1-2 - Rebuild the weak chapters. Start with whatever landed in the "weak" bucket, since these take the longest and benefit most from not being left until the end. Re-teach the concept properly rather than skimming notes, then do a small set of basic practice questions before moving on.
Weeks 3-4 - Revise everything else, chapter by chapter. Work through the "shaky" and "clear" chapters in the order they were taught, spending less time on the ones that are already solid. For each chapter: reread the summary or notes once, then close the book and write out what you remember, then check it against the notes and fix the gaps.
Week 5 - Practice under exam conditions. Solve previous years' half-yearly papers or the school's sample papers, timed, without notes. This is where concept knowledge turns into exam performance - a student can know a chapter well and still lose marks by running out of time or misreading a question, and this is the week that catches it.
Week 6 - Final revision and rest. Go back only to the topics that are still shaky after the practice papers. Avoid starting anything new in the last few days - the goal now is confidence and consolidation, not coverage. Make sure the last night before each paper is for a light recap and proper sleep, not a new topic.

Adjust the split by class: Classes 6-8 usually need less time in Week 5 on timed practice and more time on concept revision, while Classes 9-10 benefit from starting the timed practice a week earlier, since pacing a longer, board-style paper is itself a skill worth rehearsing more than once.

Subject-by-subject: where half-yearly marks are actually lost

Maths. The half-yearly usually spans several units - number systems or algebra alongside geometry or mensuration, for instance - and marks are most often lost not from not knowing a method but from applying the wrong one under time pressure, because the chapters haven't been mixed together in practice. Solve a mixed practice set that jumps between chapters, not just one chapter at a time, in the final two weeks. Science. Definitions, laws, and diagrams accumulate fast across two terms, and the half-yearly is often where a Class 8 or 9 student first has to hold several chapters of terminology in their head at once. Keep a running one-page glossary per chapter as you revise rather than trying to relearn definitions from scratch in Week 6. See our science revision tips for a chapter-by-chapter approach.

Social Studies. History timelines, geography maps, and civics concepts from the first two terms tend to blur together by exam time. Revise with a timeline or map in front of you rather than just the text, and practise writing five-mark answers in points rather than paragraphs - a habit worth building now, well before it matters even more in the Class 10 boards. Our social studies study tips guide has a chapter-wise revision method.

English. The half-yearly usually adds grammar, writing tasks, and a wider set of chapters from the literature reader or supplementary book than any unit test so far. Revise the writing formats (letters, notices, paragraphs) properly rather than assuming they're intuitive, and reread the literature chapters rather than relying on memory from when they were first taught months ago. Mistakes that turn a manageable exam into a stressful one

Starting revision from the first chapter taught instead of from the weakest chapter.
Re-reading notes and textbook chapters without ever testing recall by writing answers closed-book.
Leaving the only timed practice paper for the day before the exam, so pacing problems show up too late to fix.
Treating every chapter as equally urgent instead of triaging into clear, shaky, and weak.
Trying to start something new in the final 48 hours instead of consolidating what's already been revised.
Skipping sleep in exam week - fatigue costs more marks in a long paper than one extra hour of revision gains.

How parents can help without adding pressure

The most useful thing a parent can do in the six weeks before a half-yearly is help build the chapter list and the weekly plan, then step back and let the child work it. Asking "which chapter are we doing today?" keeps the plan visible without turning every evening into a status check. Asking "have you studied everything?" tends to produce a reassuring "yes" that isn't actually true, because it's too vague to answer honestly.

It also helps to protect the six weeks from other pressure where possible - fewer extracurricular clashes, a predictable homework and revision slot each evening, and clear boundaries around screen time without making it a daily argument. A calm, steady routine does more for exam performance than an intense final week ever does; our guide on building a CBSE study plan for the year covers how to set this rhythm up from the start of term.

Using the half-yearly as a check-up, not just a score

Whatever the result, the most valuable thing to do afterwards is go through the answer script chapter by chapter - not just note the percentage. Was a mark lost because the concept wasn't understood, because of a careless slip, or because the answer wasn't written the way the paper wanted it? Each of those has a different fix, and the half-yearly is a low-stakes, useful place to find out, with a full term still ahead before the annual exam to act on it.

For Class 9 and 10 students, this review is worth extra attention, since the half-yearly is often the closest thing to board-exam conditions a student sits before the real thing. Frequently asked questions

How is the half-yearly exam different from a unit test?

A unit test usually covers one or two chapters and is over in a week of revision. The half-yearly exam covers everything taught since the school year began - typically five to eight chapters per subject - and is closer in format and length to a board-style paper. It also usually carries a heavier weight in the report card than any single unit test, and for Class 9 and 10 students it is the first real rehearsal for how board exams are structured and marked.

When are CBSE half-yearly exams usually held?

Most CBSE schools that begin their academic year in April hold half-yearly exams in November or December, after roughly two terms of teaching. The exact dates vary by school, so always confirm against your child's school calendar or the notice sent home - but starting revision six to eight weeks before the exam gives enough runway regardless of the exact date.

How many hours a day should my child study for half-yearly exams?

For Classes 6-8, one to one and a half hours of focused revision on a school day and two to three hours on a holiday is usually enough if it starts early. For Classes 9-10, one and a half to two hours on a school day and three to four hours on a holiday is more realistic, since the syllabus is heavier and the format is closer to board papers. Consistency across six weeks matters far more than long sessions crammed into the final few days.

My child says they've revised everything but still does badly in half-yearlies. What's going wrong?

This is usually a gap between reading and recalling. Re-reading notes or a textbook chapter feels like revision but does not test whether the concept can be used under exam conditions. The fix is active revision: closing the book and writing out the answer, solving problems without looking at the worked example first, and attempting a timed practice paper before the real exam. If the gap persists even with active revision, it is often a sign of an underlying concept gap from an earlier chapter that needs to be found and fixed directly, rather than more repetition of the current one.

Should Class 9 and 10 students treat the half-yearly like a mini board exam?

Yes, and this is one of the most useful ways to use it. Many CBSE schools now set half-yearly papers in the competency-based, case-study format used in board exams, and the exam is a low-stakes chance to practise reading time, pacing a longer paper, and answer-writing discipline - skipping steps, missing word limits, misreading command words - well before those mistakes cost marks in the actual boards. Reviewing the half-yearly answer script afterwards is often more useful than the score itself.

What should parents do if half-yearly results are weaker than expected?

Treat the result as information, not a verdict. Go through the answer script chapter by chapter with your child to see exactly where marks were lost - a concept never understood, a careless error, or an answer-writing habit - rather than reacting to the overall percentage. A result that is weaker than expected in October or November still leaves a full term before the annual exam to fix the specific gap, which is exactly the kind of targeted, low-pressure problem outside academic support is best suited to solve.
