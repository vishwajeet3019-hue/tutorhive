Classes 6-8

Why Marks Drop in Class 6, 7 and 8, and What Parents Can Do About It

In Class 5 the report card was full of A grades and the only complaint was handwriting. By Class 7 the same child is bringing home 60s, says "I'm just bad at maths", and nothing obvious has changed. The change is real, though, and it is common enough to have a name, the middle school dip. It is rarely about laziness or a sudden loss of ability. It is usually a handful of specific, findable causes, and this guide helps you work out which ones apply to your child.

The short answer

Marks drop in Classes 6 to 8 because school asks for different skills at the same time as it gives children less hand-holding. Subjects multiply, maths becomes abstract, science and social science turn into separate disciplines, and answers get longer. Any small gap left over from the primary years starts to show. Add an age where friends, phones and sleep begin to compete with study, and a drop is almost predictable. The fix is to find the actual cause, one subject at a time, before adding hours or tuition.

What actually changes after Class 5

It helps to see the jump from your child's side. A few things shift at once, and most children manage one or two of them but not all together.

TeachersOne class teacher who knows each child's notebookA different teacher for each subject, each seeing many more students

A general picture; the exact syllabus varies by board and school.

None of these is a problem alone. Together they explain why a child who was coping in Class 5 can feel, in Class 7, as if the ground has moved.

Seven common causes, and how each one looks at home

1. A gap from the primary years that was never fixed

This is the quiet one. Everything up to Class 5 was passable even with a shaky grip on tables, place value or fractions, because the questions were short and the teacher was close by. In Class 6 and 7 those same skills are the foundation for the new work, so one missing brick makes several chapters wobble.

The national data suggests this is not rare. ASER 2024, a large survey of rural India, found that only about 46% of Class 8 children could do a division problem (3-digit by 1-digit), an operation covered in the primary years, and that the figure has barely moved since 2018. ASER surveys rural households, so urban figures are likely to be higher, but the mechanism it points to, early gaps surviving into upper classes, can happen in any school.

At home it looks like: one subject, usually maths, that has been getting worse gradually; slow calculation; a child who can follow the teacher but cannot do the homework alone.

2. The subject itself became abstract

Maths stops being about counting things and starts being about letters standing for numbers. Science starts using words like "photosynthesis" and "pressure" that have to be understood, not just remembered. A child who relied on picturing the answer in Class 5 now has to reason with ideas they cannot see.

At home it looks like: "I understood it in class but I can't do it now", and a drop that is sharpest in the new topic of the term, such as algebra or force and pressure.

3. Memorising worked until it didn't

Many children get through the primary years by learning answers by heart, because the tests ask for exactly what the book says. In Classes 6 to 8 more questions ask them to apply an idea in a new context, and memorised answers stop working. We explain how schools are moving this way in our guide to CBSE competency-based questions.

At home it looks like: good marks in a test that follows the textbook closely, and poor marks the moment the question is phrased differently. "It was not in the book" is a common complaint.

4. Longer reading and writing

Class 6 papers have more words in the questions and more words expected in the answers. A child who reads slowly, writes slowly or struggles to organise a paragraph loses marks to an unfinished paper, even when they know the content.

At home it looks like: blank answers at the end of the paper, rushed handwriting in the last pages, short and vague written answers despite knowing the topic when you ask them aloud.

5. Less supervision, so problems hide

With subject teachers and bigger classes, a child can quietly fall behind for weeks before anyone notices. Many parents also step back at this age, with good intentions, to encourage independence. The result is that small problems grow unobserved until the first term result.

At home it looks like: incomplete notebooks, homework "finished" with no detail, a child who stopped asking doubts in class because they did not want to look slow.

6. Sleep, screens and distractions

Between roughly 11 and 14, many children get their first phone, join gaming groups and start staying up later. The American Academy of Sleep Medicine recommends 9 to 12 hours of sleep for children aged 6 to 12, and 8 to 10 for teenagers aged 13 to 18. A child who sleeps six and a half hours is tired, forgetful and easily frustrated, which looks exactly like not trying.

At home it looks like: a drop across all subjects at once, morning struggles, a child who is irritable or tearful over small things, and homework that takes two hours of "studying" with little to show.

7. Confidence and the story they tell about themselves

After a first poor result, many children conclude "I'm bad at maths" or "I'm just not a studying person", and then stop trying, which confirms the story. Comparison with siblings or classmates, and changes in friendships at this age, make it worse.

At home it looks like: sweeping statements about themselves, avoiding one subject, hiding test papers, or acting as if they do not care.

Rule these out first

Before assuming any of the causes above, check the basics: eyesight (a child who cannot read the board will fall behind in every subject), hearing, sleep, and whether there has been any change at school such as a new friendship group or bullying.

If your child works hard but keeps struggling with one specific skill, such as reading fluently, writing legibly or handling numbers, ask the school counsellor whether an assessment for a learning difference would be sensible. Early identification makes a large difference.

A 20-minute way to find the real cause

You do not need to know the subject. Sit with your child's last three corrected tests, in the subject that worries you most, and sort every lost mark into one of five buckets:

  1. Did not know it. The topic was never understood.
  2. Knew it but used it wrongly. The method was memorised but the idea was not.
  3. Misread the question. Answered something that was not asked.
  4. Careless. The method was right; the arithmetic or copying was not.
  5. Ran out of time. The paper was incomplete.

Then match the pattern to a likely cause:

What you seeLikely causeFirst move
One subject, slowly worse over a yearA foundation gap (cause 1) or the subject becoming abstract (cause 2)Find the earliest topic they cannot do, and rebuild from there

Most children have two or three of these overlapping. Pick the one that explains the most lost marks and start there. If marks are mainly being lost to unfamiliar-style questions, our guide on how to improve your child's maths marks shows how to build that practice.

A two-week plan that does not turn into a battle

Week 1: understand before you act

  • Have one calm conversation. Choose a relaxed moment, not right after a bad test. Try: "I have noticed maths has been harder lately. I am not upset, I just want to understand what is going on. What part feels hardest?" Then listen without correcting.
  • Talk to the class teacher. Ask for two or three specific examples of where marks are being lost. Our guide to questions to ask at a parent-teacher meeting gives you a list.
  • Run the 20-minute diagnosis above on one subject only.

Week 2: one small, visible fix

  • Choose one subject and one gap. Fixing three things at once produces three half-fixes.
  • Use short daily sessions. Thirty focused minutes at the same time every day beats a two-hour weekend rescue. Start with the easiest part of the gap so the first sessions end in success.
  • Keep a win log. One line a day: "solved fractions with unlike denominators without help". Visible progress rebuilds the confidence that cause 7 wears down.
  • Review after two weeks. Look at what changed in the child's work, not only the next test score.

For general routines that make this easier, see our guides on building a homework routine and study habits for school students.

What tends to make it worse

  • Comparing. "Look at how Aarav is doing" reliably lowers a child's effort, not raises it.
  • Adding hours without a diagnosis. A child who has a gap in fractions does not need two more hours of the same chapter.
  • Taking away everything they enjoy. Cutting sport or friends removes the things that keep a child resilient, and links study with punishment.
  • Sitting beside them and solving problems. It is efficient in the moment and teaches them to wait for help.
  • Hiring tuition for every subject at once. It overloads the child and makes it impossible to tell what is working.
  • Switching schools as a reflex. A school change is a big disruption; try finding the cause first.

When tuition helps, and when it does not

Tuition helps most when the cause is a skill gap (causes 1, 2 and 3), because a patient tutor can rebuild the missing step, explain it in a different way from school, and give targeted practice that a busy parent usually cannot. It also helps when a child has stopped asking doubts in class and needs a low-pressure place to ask.

It does not help much when the real cause is sleep, screens or something happening at school. Adding classes on top of an exhausted child only makes the exhaustion worse. That is why it is worth doing the diagnosis first. Our guide to the signs your child needs extra tuition and the one on choosing online tuition when marks are dropping help you judge which side of that line you are on.

If you do try tuition, begin with one subject and judge it after six to eight weeks, by more than the next test score. Is your child asking questions? Do they attempt unfamiliar problems? Do they finish timed sections? Those are the early signs that it is working.

Frequently asked questions

Is it normal for marks to drop when a child moves to Class 6?

Yes, a dip is very common. The move to subject teachers, longer answers, more abstract maths and less supervision happens all at once. Many children recover within a term or two once the cause is identified, but a drop that continues for a full year usually points to a specific gap or habit that needs attention.

Which class is the hardest in middle school?

It varies by child, but Class 6 is hard because of the transition itself, and Class 8 is hard because it sets up the jump to Class 9. In maths, algebra and ratio in Classes 7 and 8 are where gaps from earlier years often start to show.

How long does it take to recover lost marks?

A single gap, such as fractions or a particular topic, can often be fixed in four to eight weeks of short, focused daily work. A drop caused by habits, sleep or confidence takes longer because the routine has to change first. Judge progress by the child's work and confidence, not only by the next test score.

How much should a child in Classes 6 to 8 study every day?

As a rough guide, about one to two hours a day including homework, rising slightly from Class 6 to Class 8. More important than the number is that the time is focused, at a fixed hour, and with a clear task. Two hours of distracted studying is worth less than 45 focused minutes.

Should I hire a tutor for every subject that has dropped?

Usually no. Start with the one subject where the drop is largest or the gap is clearest, and judge it after six to eight weeks. Other subjects often improve when sleep, routine and confidence improve, without needing separate tuition.

Should I change my child's school if marks keep dropping?

Rarely as a first step. Most middle school dips come from skill gaps, routine and confidence, which follow a child to a new school. Speak with the class teacher, check sleep and health, and try a focused plan for a term first. A school change makes sense mainly when the problem is clearly the environment, such as bullying or a persistent mismatch.
