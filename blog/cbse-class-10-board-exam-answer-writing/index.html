CBSE Class 10 Board Exam Answer-Writing: How to Turn What You Know Into Marks

Every year, students walk out of a board exam saying they knew everything - and still come back with marks lower than they expected. The gap is almost never the syllabus. It is the answer: a Maths step skipped, a science keyword missing, a five-mark question answered in one paragraph, a word limit ignored, a command word misread. Content gets you ready to answer; answer-writing is what actually converts that into a score. This guide covers the rules that apply to every paper, then goes subject by subject, and ends with how to manage the three-hour paper itself.

Why marks leak even when the preparation is solid

Think of a board script from the examiner's side. They are marking hundreds of papers against a fixed marking scheme that lists the exact points, steps and keywords each answer must contain. They award a mark when they can see the required point clearly. They cannot award a mark for something you knew but did not write, wrote in a way they could not find, or answered a different question than the one asked.

That is where the marks go. Not in a topic the student never studied, but in eight, ten, twelve marks scattered across the paper - a missing formula line here, a one-paragraph answer to a five-point question there, a diagram left unlabelled, a "distinguish between" question answered as a single description. Added up, that is often the difference between the mark a student expected and the one they got.

The good news is that this is a skill, and it responds quickly to practice. In the last few months before boards, tightening answer-writing usually raises marks faster than covering more content does, because the content is mostly already there.

Know the paper you are actually writing

For most main subjects, the CBSE Class 10 board theory paper is 80 marks, with 20 marks of internal assessment handled by the school. You get three hours to write it, plus 15 minutes of reading time before you are allowed to start writing.

The paper is built from a mix of question types: objective questions (multiple choice, assertion-reason, very short answer), short-answer questions, long-answer questions, and case-based or source-based sets that give you a passage, a diagram or data and ask several questions on it. A large share of the paper - close to half in recent years - is made up of competency-based questions that test whether you can apply a concept to an unfamiliar situation, not just recall it.

The exact number of questions per section, and the marks split, is revised by CBSE from time to time. Before you build your strategy, download the official CBSE sample paper and marking scheme for your subject and year and read the structure carefully, so nothing about the layout is a surprise on exam day.

The rules that apply to every paper

Read the command word and answer that. "Define", "state", "explain", "describe", "distinguish between", "justify", "derive", "calculate" and "with reference to the context" all ask for different things. "State two reasons" wants two reasons, briefly - not a paragraph on the topic. "Distinguish between" wants a point-by-point comparison, ideally in two columns, not two separate descriptions. Misreading the command word is one of the most common ways a well-prepared student loses marks on a question they could have answered fully.

Match the length to the mark value. One mark is a word or a line. Two to three marks is three or four sentences or points. Five marks is five or six distinct points, each developed into a sentence, with a heading, diagram or example where the subject allows. Writing a page for a two-mark question earns nothing extra and steals time from the rest of the paper.

Make the marks easy to find. Write the question number clearly and in the margin. Start each answer on a new line. Answer in points, not dense blocks. Underline keywords, technical terms and the final result. If an examiner has to hunt through an unbroken paragraph for the point that earns the mark, some of those points will be missed.

Attempt everything. There is no negative marking. A blank is a guaranteed zero; an attempt is a chance at partial or step marks. For objective questions you are unsure of, eliminate what you can and choose - never leave one empty.

Use the internal choice deliberately. Where a question offers "or", read both options fully before choosing, then commit. Do not answer both and expect the examiner to pick the better one - the instructions usually say the first attempt is marked.

Keep the paper navigable. Answer questions in any order you like, but keep them in numerical order on the page as far as possible, and if you leave a question to come back to, leave a clearly marked gap rather than squeezing it in later or writing it out of sequence at the end without a note.

Maths: show the examiner every step

Maths is marked in parts. A long-answer question typically carries marks for the correct formula or statement, the correct substitution of values, the working, and the final answer with units. Write only the final answer - even the correct one - and you usually lose most of the marks for that question. Set out every step and make one slip near the end, and you keep the method marks.

A reliable structure for each solution:

Write Given and To find (or To prove) at the top.
State the formula or theorem you are using before you use it.
Show the substitution of values into the formula.
Show each line of working - do not do two steps in your head and jump.
Box or underline the final answer, and write the unit.

For geometry, draw a clean figure in pencil even when the question does not ask for one, and mark the given information on it. In construction questions, leave all arcs and construction lines visible - erasing them removes the evidence the examiner marks. For "prove that" questions, write Given, To prove, Construction and Proof as separate labelled parts, and give a reason for each statement.

Where a result can be checked - an equation solved, an identity proved - spend ten seconds verifying it. And carry units through the whole calculation, not just the last line. Science: precise definitions, labelled diagrams, balanced equations

Definitions and statements of law must be exact. The marking scheme looks for specific keywords. "Something that speeds up a reaction" will not earn the mark that "a substance that alters the rate of a chemical reaction without being consumed in it" earns. Learn the standard wording for definitions, laws and principles, and reproduce it.

Diagrams carry marks of their own. Draw them large, in pencil, with a ruler for straight lines, and label every part with a straight, non-crossing guideline. An unlabelled diagram usually earns nothing; a labelled one can carry two or three marks on its own. Common ones - the human eye, the reflex arc, the nephron, the reproductive systems, ray diagrams for lenses and mirrors, electrolysis and the experimental set-ups - are worth practising until they are quick and clean.

Chemical equations must be balanced and state-marked. Write the balanced equation, include the physical states (s), (l), (g), (aq), and note conditions like heat or a catalyst above the arrow. An unbalanced equation loses part of the mark even when the reactants and products are right.

Numericals follow the Maths structure: formula, substitution, working, answer, unit. Physics numericals in electricity, light and work-energy are predictable marks if the steps are all shown.

Reason-based answers need the "because". When a question asks "why", the mark is in the causal link, so write it explicitly rather than just restating the phenomenon. Social Science: points, not paragraphs

Social Science is where the single-paragraph habit costs the most. A five-mark question in History, Geography, Political Science or Economics wants five distinct points, each on its own line, each developed into a sentence, ideally with a short heading in the margin or underlined. One flowing paragraph that contains three of those points earns three marks at best, because the other points are not there in a form the examiner can tick.

Source-based questions: read the extract first, then the questions, then the extract again with the questions in mind. The answers are anchored in the passage - quote or paraphrase the relevant line and then explain it. Do not answer from general knowledge and ignore the source.

Map work: use a sharp pencil, locate features precisely, use the correct symbols, and write names clearly along the feature. Practise the prescribed list of History and Geography map items until marking them is automatic - these are among the easiest marks in the paper to secure and to throw away.

Dates, terms and names matter in History and Political Science, and diagrams and examples lift Geography and Economics answers. English: follow the format, respect the word limit

The English papers reward structure and accuracy more than volume.

Writing tasks have fixed formats. A formal letter, an analytical paragraph, a notice - each has a layout that carries marks: sender's address, date, receiver, subject line, salutation, body, closing. Learn each format and follow it exactly. Content, expression and accuracy are marked separately, so a well-organised answer with a few grammar slips still scores.

Stay within the word limit. If the question says 100-120 words, write around that. Markers are instructed not to reward large overshoots, and a long answer eats time. Plan two or three points in the margin before you start writing so the paragraph has a shape.

Reference-to-context and literature answers should name the poem or chapter and the writer, answer the specific sub-question, and stay close to the text. For "your view" questions, take a clear position and support it with one or two reasons.

Grammar and reading sections are pure accuracy - do the editing, gap-fill and comprehension carefully and re-read your answers. Managing the three-hour paper

Use the 15 minutes of reading time properly. Read the whole paper. Mark the questions you are sure of, the ones you will do carefully, and the ones to leave for last. Decide your order. Note which questions have internal choice so you are not deciding under time pressure later.

Do the objective section first, fast. These are quick, definite marks. Get them down in the first 20-25 minutes and move on with momentum.

Budget time by marks. Roughly two minutes per mark leaves a buffer. Long-answer questions get the time their marks justify - no more. If a question is running long, leave a gap, move on, and come back if time allows. One perfect answer is worth the same as one adequate answer; finishing the paper is worth far more than polishing one question.

Keep the last 15 minutes for review. Check that every question is attempted and numbered, that no part is missing, that final answers have units, that diagrams are labelled, and that the objective answers match what you intended.

If a question makes your mind go blank, move to one you know, get some marks on the page, and come back - the panic usually passes once the pen is moving. Our guide to managing board exam stress covers exam-day nerves in more detail.

Common answer-writing mistakes that cost marks

Writing only the final answer in Maths and skipping the steps that carry the marks.
Answering a five-mark question in one paragraph instead of five clear points.
Loose, non-standard wording for definitions and laws, missing the keyword the scheme wants.
Diagrams that are small, drawn in pen, or left unlabelled.
Unbalanced chemical equations, or equations with no state symbols.
Ignoring the word limit and format in English writing tasks.
Misreading the command word - describing when the question said distinguish, or listing when it said justify.
Attempting both options of an internal choice instead of committing to one.
Leaving objective questions blank when there is no negative marking.
Spending 20 minutes making one answer perfect and then running out of time for two others.
Messy question numbering, so the examiner cannot match an answer to a question.

How to build the skill before the boards

Answer-writing improves through marked practice, not through reading about it. Through the pre-board period, sit full past papers and official sample papers in a single three-hour block, by hand, no notes. Then mark your own script line by line against the CBSE marking scheme and write down exactly where each lost mark went - a skipped step, a missing keyword, a format error, a misread command word. Patterns show up within two or three papers.

Treat the school pre-boards as the dress rehearsal for all of this - not just for the syllabus, but for pacing, presentation and nerves. A weak pre-board that shows you where the answer-writing is leaking is doing its job; our month-by-month preparation timeline and guide to scoring 90% in Class 10 both build this rehearsal into the schedule.

A teacher or tutor who marks the way a board examiner does - looking for keywords, steps and structure rather than a general sense that the student "gets it" - shortens the process considerably, because students almost never notice their own presentation habits.

Frequently asked questions

My child knows the syllabus but keeps losing marks in exams. Why?

In most cases it is an answer-writing problem, not a knowledge problem. Marks leak in predictable places: skipping steps in Maths so the examiner cannot award step marks, writing a science definition loosely so a keyword is missing, answering a five-mark Social Science question in one paragraph instead of five clear points, ignoring the word limit and format in English writing tasks, and misreading the command word so the answer is off-target. Fixing these is a skill built through timed practice checked against the official marking scheme, and it usually adds marks faster in the last few months than covering more content does.

How does step marking work in CBSE Class 10 Maths?

Long-answer Maths questions are marked in parts: the correct formula or statement, the correct substitution of values, the working, and the final answer with units each carry marks. A student who writes only the final answer, even the right one, usually loses most of the marks for that question. A student who sets out every step but makes one arithmetic slip near the end still keeps the method marks. This is why showing full working - given, to find, formula, substitution, steps, boxed answer, units - matters even when the sum feels obvious.

What is the CBSE Class 10 board exam pattern for 2027?

For most main subjects the board theory paper is 80 marks with 20 marks of internal assessment, written in three hours with 15 minutes of reading time before the paper starts. Papers mix objective questions (multiple choice, assertion-reason, very short answer), short-answer questions, long-answer questions and case-based or source-based sets, with a large share of the paper - close to half in recent years - made up of competency-based questions that test application rather than recall. Always confirm the exact section structure and marks against the official CBSE sample paper and marking scheme for your subject and year, since the split is revised from time to time.

How much should a student write for a three-mark or five-mark question?

Match the answer to the mark value, not to how much you know. A rough guide: one mark is a word or a line, two to three marks is three to four sentences or points, and five marks is five or six distinct points, each developed in a sentence, ideally with a heading, a diagram or an example where the subject allows. Writing a page for a two-mark question wastes time you need elsewhere and rarely earns anything extra; writing two lines for a five-mark question guarantees lost marks.

Do neatness and presentation actually change board exam marks?

Handwriting is not marked, but legibility and structure are. An examiner scoring hundreds of scripts awards marks for points they can find quickly. Clear question numbers, a fresh line for each answer, underlined keywords, point-wise answers instead of dense blocks, labelled diagrams drawn in pencil, and a boxed final answer all make the marks easy to award. Messy, unbroken paragraphs where a correct point is buried mid-sentence are where earned marks quietly disappear.

How can a student practise answer-writing before the boards?

Sit full past papers and official sample papers in one three-hour block, by hand, with no notes, then mark your own script line by line against the CBSE marking scheme and note exactly where marks were lost. Do this for every subject through the pre-board period and treat the pre-boards themselves as the dress rehearsal. Having answers checked by a teacher or tutor who marks the way a board examiner does - looking for keywords, steps and structure - shortens this process, because students rarely spot their own presentation habits.
