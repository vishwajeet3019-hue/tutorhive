15 CBSE Class 10 Study Tips Toppers Use That Most Students Skip

Two students can put in the exact same number of hours and walk out with a 20-mark gap between them. It's rarely talent. It's almost always method — toppers tend to revise in a handful of specific, slightly unglamorous ways that most students have heard of but never actually built into a habit. None of these need extra tuition, extra hours, or extra money. They need to replace what you're already doing with something that works better. Here are 15 of them, grouped so you can start with the ones that will move your marks fastest.

Why "studying more" isn't actually the toppers' secret

Ask most Class 10 students how to improve marks and the answer is almost always "study more hours." It's the most common advice and, on its own, one of the least useful — because a student who reads the same chapter for the fifth time isn't learning much more than they did on the fourth. What actually separates the student scoring 96% from the one scoring 76% is rarely raw effort. Both are usually working hard. The difference is that one of them is testing what they know, revising on a schedule instead of once, and fixing the exact reason behind every wrong answer — while the other is reading, highlighting, and hoping it sticks.

The tips below aren't secret. Most toppers will tell you exactly what they do if you ask. The reason they still work as "secrets" is that almost nobody builds them into a real routine — they stay as things you know you should do, not things you actually do every week. Pick two or three to start tonight rather than trying all 15 at once.

The revision techniques toppers actually use

1. Test yourself before you check the answer. This is called active recall, and it's the single highest-leverage change most students can make. Instead of re-reading a chapter, close the book and write down everything you remember about it — definitions, steps, diagrams, dates — then open the book and check what you missed. It feels harder than re-reading because it exposes gaps immediately, which is exactly why it works: re-reading only feels like learning.

2. Revise on a schedule, not once. A chapter revised once in September and never touched again is mostly forgotten by December, even if it felt solid at the time. Toppers build in short repeat passes — a quick recall test a few days after first revising a chapter, then again after two weeks, then again a month later. Each pass takes less time than the last because the memory is stronger, but skipping the repeat passes is why "I studied this already" so often turns into "I don't remember any of this" by exam time.

3. Explain it out loud as if teaching it. Pick a concept, then explain it in your own words to an empty room, a younger sibling, or a parent who has no idea what a quadratic equation is. The moment you get stuck or start using vague words like "it's basically when..." you've found the exact part you don't actually understand yet — something re-reading notes almost never reveals.

4. Solve previous years' papers before hunting for "important questions." Lists of predicted or "most important" questions that circulate before every board exam are usually guesses. Actual previous years' papers, solved under a timer with no notes, show you the real pattern: how questions are phrased, how marks are distributed, and where the paper tends to be tighter on time than expected. Our CBSE Class 10 sample papers guide covers how to build this into your final months.

5. Keep a mistake notebook, not just a notes notebook. Every time you get a question wrong in practice — a silly slip, a formula mix-up, a misread question — write it in one running notebook with a one-line note on why it happened. Read through this notebook the week before each exam instead of your entire syllabus again. It's usually two or three pages, and it directly targets the errors you are most likely to repeat.

Smaller daily habits that quietly add marks

6. Follow a two-day rule before every exam. Stop starting new topics 48 hours before an exam, no matter how tempting it feels to squeeze in "just one more chapter." The last two days go to consolidating what you already know and getting proper sleep. A half-learned new topic rarely earns marks; a well-rested brain recalling what it already knows usually does.

7. Put your phone in another room while studying. Not on silent, not face-down — in another room. Studies on attention consistently show that a phone within reach reduces focus even when it's never touched, because part of your attention stays on guard for it. A single 45-minute block with your phone genuinely out of reach usually beats two hours of studying with it next to you.

8. Treat sleep as part of revision, not time stolen from it. Memory consolidation — the process that moves what you studied today into long-term memory — happens mainly during sleep. Cutting sleep to fit in one more hour of revision often means that hour, and some of the day's earlier hours, are retained more weakly than they would have been with proper rest. If exam stress is making sleep genuinely difficult rather than just tempting to skip, our guide to managing board exam stress covers that in more depth.

9. Study in focused blocks, not open-ended sessions. "I'll study science for a while" tends to drift. "I'll do 25 focused minutes on light and reflection, then a 5-minute break" doesn't. Short, defined blocks with a clear goal and a real break in between produce more retained material per hour than long, vague sessions where attention quietly fades after the first 20 minutes.

10. Do your hardest subject first, not last. Willpower and focus are highest earlier in a study session and drop as it goes on. Most students save the subject they like least for the end, when concentration is already low — which is exactly backwards. Starting with the subject you find hardest, while focus is freshest, is a small change that consistently produces better results than starting with whatever feels easiest.

Subject-specific shortcuts that make revision faster

11. Maths: solve first, check the solution second. Reading a worked example and nodding along feels like understanding, but the real test is solving the problem cold, without looking at the method first. If you get stuck, that's the moment to check the solution — not before attempting it. This single change is often the difference between recognising a method and being able to apply it under exam pressure.

12. Science: turn definitions and diagrams into a flashcard habit. Physics, chemistry, and biology accumulate a large number of exact definitions, laws, and labelled diagrams across the year, and they're easy to blur together by exam time. Keep a running set of flashcards — physical or in a notes app — and go through them for five minutes most days rather than trying to relearn all of them in the final month.

13. Social Science and English: practise writing in points, not paragraphs of thought. A well-organised, point-wise answer with clear headings usually scores higher than a well-written paragraph covering the same facts, because examiners are marking against a points-based scheme. Practise converting what you know into short, labelled points before the exam, not during it.

The myths that quietly hold good students back

14. An all-nighter before the exam almost never helps. It feels like extra preparation, but the research on sleep and memory is fairly one-sided: a tired brain recalls less accurately, makes more careless errors, and processes new information worse than a rested one. If a topic isn't ready the night before an exam, an hour of calm revision and a full night's sleep beats staying up to force it in.

15. More tuition without a clear target rarely closes a specific gap. Adding hours of coaching sounds like a solution, but if it re-teaches everything from the start rather than targeting the exact chapter or question type that's actually weak, it mostly adds hours without adding marks. The tips above work because they're specific — test the exact thing you're unsure of, fix the exact mistake you made, revise the exact chapter that's shaky. Support works the same way only when it's built around the same kind of specificity.

How to actually start tonight

Fifteen tips is too many to start at once, and trying all of them together usually means none of them stick. Pick three: one from the revision list, one daily habit, and your mistake notebook. Run those for two weeks before adding anything else. The goal isn't to do everything on this page — it's to permanently replace re-reading and last-minute cramming with methods that actually hold up under exam pressure. Frequently asked questions

What is the single biggest difference between toppers and average students?

It's rarely the number of hours studied. The consistent difference is how the time is used: toppers test themselves instead of re-reading, revise on a schedule instead of cramming once, and fix the specific mistake behind a wrong answer instead of moving on. A student who studies four focused, tested hours a day usually retains more than one who reads for seven hours without ever closing the book to check.

What is active recall and why does it work better than re-reading notes?

Active recall means closing the book and trying to write or say everything you remember about a topic before checking it against your notes. Re-reading feels productive because the material looks familiar, but familiarity is not the same as being able to reproduce it under exam pressure without the page in front of you. Active recall exposes exactly which parts you actually know and which parts only felt known, which is precisely what a board exam tests.

Is group study helpful or harmful for CBSE Class 10 preparation?

It depends entirely on structure. An unstructured group session — sitting together with books open — usually turns into a longer, less focused version of studying alone. A structured session, where each person prepares a chapter and explains it to the others, or where the group tests each other with questions and closes books to answer, genuinely helps because explaining a concept forces the same active recall that works so well solo. If a session doesn't have a plan for what each person will explain or be tested on, it's better to study alone and save group time for doubt-clearing only.

How far in advance should revision start before CBSE Class 10 boards?

The techniques on this page matter more than the exact start date, but as a general guide, dedicated revision works best starting two to three months before the boards, with the final two weeks reserved only for consolidation and timed practice papers, not new topics. For a full week-by-week breakdown, see our CBSE Class 10 study timetable guide.

Do previous years' question papers actually help more than guessing important questions?

Yes, and by a wide margin. Lists of "important questions" that circulate before exams are usually guesses with no real basis, and relying on them means gambling on which chapters will appear. Previous years' papers, solved under timed conditions and then marked against the official CBSE scheme, show the actual pattern of question types, marking language, and pacing — information no guessed list can give you. Our guide to CBSE Class 10 sample papers covers exactly how to build this into a study plan.

Why do some students study for hours every day but still score average marks?

This usually comes down to one of three things: revising by re-reading instead of testing recall, studying every subject the same way instead of adjusting method to what the subject actually demands, or never reviewing mistakes closely enough to stop repeating them. More hours on top of the same method mostly produces more tiredness, not more marks — the fix is almost always to change how the hours are spent, not to add more of them.
